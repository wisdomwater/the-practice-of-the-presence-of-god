## Sixth Letter — Finding the Soul’s Place of Rest

*To the Reverend Father*  
*Undated*

My Reverend Father,

I have not found my particular way of life described in the books I have read. Although I feel no inward uneasiness about it, I would welcome your judgment for greater assurance. I therefore wish to explain something of how I came to live as I do.

A few days ago, a devout person told me that the spiritual life unfolds through grace, beginning with the fear of punishment, growing through the hope of eternal life, and reaching its fulfillment in pure love. He explained that each of these conditions contains different stages through which a person advances toward that blessed fulfillment. I have not followed this progression. For reasons I cannot fully explain, such methods discouraged me.

When I entered religious life, I resolved instead to give myself entirely to God, believing this surrender to be the best amends I could offer for my sins. For His love, I determined to renounce everything besides Him. During the first years, I usually spent my appointed times of devotion reflecting upon death, judgment, hell, heaven, and my sins. Throughout the rest of the day, even in the midst of work, I carefully directed my attention toward God, whom I understood to be always with me and often contemplated as dwelling within me.

Gradually, almost without noticing the change, I began to practice this same attention during my formal prayer. It brought me great delight and consolation. Through it, my reverence for God grew so deep that faith alone could satisfy what I sought to know of Him.

Yet I must tell you that my first ten years were filled with suffering. I feared that I had not given myself to God as completely as I desired, while the memory of my past sins remained continually before me. The great favors He bestowed upon me, which I knew I had done nothing to deserve, also became a source of anguish. During those years, I fell often and rose again just as often.

It seemed that creation, reason, and even God Himself stood against me, with faith alone remaining on my side. Sometimes I feared that my belief in having received such favors was merely presumption, as though I imagined myself already possessing what others attained only through long struggle. At other times, I suspected that I was deliberately deceiving myself and that there could be no salvation for me. Through all this distress, however, my trust in God remained, and my faith continued to deepen.

I had come to expect that I would spend the rest of my life in this troubled condition when, all at once, something changed. My soul entered a profound inward peace. It was as though it had finally discovered its own center and resting place.

Since then, I have continued walking before God in simple faith, humility, and love. I try diligently to avoid doing, saying, or thinking anything that might displease Him. Having done whatever I can, I hope to leave Him free to do with me as He pleases.

What I experience now is difficult to express. I feel neither distress nor doubt about my condition, because I desire only God's will and endeavor to fulfill it in everything. My surrender is such that I would not wish to lift a straw from the ground against His direction or from any motive other than love for Him.

I have set aside particular devotional methods and prescribed prayers, except those required by my religious vocation. My chief concern is to persevere in God's holy presence through a simple attentiveness and a deeply absorbing, loving regard for Him. I might call this an immediate awareness of His presence, though perhaps it is better described as a silent, hidden, continual communion between the soul and God. It often brings such great inward joy and rapture, sometimes finding outward expression as well, that I must take steps to restrain what others might notice.

I am certain beyond doubt that my soul has lived with God in this way for more than thirty years. I will leave many things unsaid so as not to weary you. Nevertheless, I would like to explain how I sometimes understand my relationship with Him, whom I behold as my King.

I imagine myself as the most wretched of people, covered in wounds and corruption, guilty of every kind of offense against my King. With deep sorrow, I confess my wrongdoing, ask His forgiveness, and place myself entirely in His hands to do with as He pleases. Yet this King, overflowing with mercy and goodness, receives me with an embrace instead of punishment. He seats me at His table, serves me with His own hands, and gives me the key to His treasures.

He speaks with me continually and takes delight in my company in more ways than I can count. In every respect, He treats me as His beloved favorite. This is one of the ways I sometimes place myself before Him in prayer.

More often, I remain in that simple attention and absorbing love of which I have already spoken. I frequently find myself held to God with a sweetness and delight greater than that of an infant resting at its mother's breast. If I may venture to use such language, I would call this resting in the bosom of God, because of the inexpressible tenderness I experience there. When necessity or weakness draws my thoughts away, I am soon recalled by inward movements so lovely and delightful that I feel embarrassed even to mention them.

Please give more consideration to my great misery, which you know so well, than to the extraordinary kindness God shows me. I remain unworthy and ungrateful despite all He gives.

My appointed hours of prayer continue this same communion. Sometimes I imagine myself as a stone in the hands of a sculptor who intends to make a statue. Presenting myself to God in this way, I ask Him to make me wholly like Himself and to form His perfect image within my soul. At other times, as soon as I begin to pray, my whole spirit rises toward Him without effort and remains there, held steady in God as its center and resting place.

I know that some people describe this condition as inactivity, delusion, or self-love. I acknowledge that there is a holy inactivity in it. And if self-love were possible within such complete absorption in God, it would surely be a happy kind. In this repose, the soul cannot occupy itself with the repeated acts that formerly sustained it; what once helped would now disturb rather than strengthen its communion.

Yet I cannot accept that this should be called delusion, for the soul desires only God in what it experiences. If I am nevertheless deceived, I leave it to Him to correct me. May He do with me whatever He pleases. My only desire is for Him and to belong wholly to Him.

Even so, you would do me a great kindness by sending your opinion. I receive your judgment with deep respect and hold you in particular esteem.

Yours in our Lord, my Reverend Father.
