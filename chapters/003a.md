## First Conversation — Awakening to the Presence of God

*August 3, 1666*

I first met Brother Lawrence on August 3, 1666. During our conversation, he spoke of a remarkable experience he had received from God at the age of eighteen, an experience that had profoundly changed the direction of his life. It began during the winter, when he noticed a tree standing bare, its branches stripped of their leaves. As he considered its appearance, he remembered that spring would eventually return, bringing new leaves, followed by blossoms and fruit.

In this simple observation, he suddenly became aware of the extraordinary providence and power of God. The tree, seemingly lifeless in the cold of winter, held within it the promise of renewal, sustained by a wisdom and power beyond its own. This realization awakened in him such a deep love for God that the experience remained vivid throughout the following forty years. Indeed, he could scarcely say whether his love had grown during those decades, so completely had that first encounter transformed his heart and loosened his attachment to the world.

Brother Lawrence also spoke openly about his own limitations. Before entering the monastery, he had worked as a servant to Monsieur Fieubert, a treasurer, and described himself as an awkward man who seemed constantly to break whatever he handled. When he sought admission to the monastery, he expected that his clumsiness and many shortcomings would bring him considerable hardship. He imagined that the discipline of religious life would provide opportunities to suffer for his faults, yet he found himself surprised by God, for his experience in the monastery had instead been one of deep satisfaction.

As our conversation continued, Brother Lawrence explained that the spiritual life consisted largely in cultivating an ongoing awareness of God's presence. He encouraged us to speak with God continually, bringing our thoughts, concerns, and activities into an intimate and familiar conversation with the One who is always near. He regarded it as a serious loss when we abandoned this communion to occupy ourselves with trivial distractions and foolish concerns. Rather than allowing our minds to become consumed by passing things, he believed we should nourish our souls through contemplation of God's greatness, goodness, and majesty, discovering joy in the simple act of belonging to Him.

He spoke particularly about the importance of faith, which he believed needed to be awakened and strengthened within us. He lamented that so many people possessed so little living faith, choosing instead to occupy themselves with minor religious practices that changed from one day to the next. For Brother Lawrence, these outward devotions could never replace the deeper orientation of a heart genuinely trusting in God. He believed that the way of faith lay at the very heart of the Church's spiritual life and was sufficient to lead a person toward the fullness of Christian perfection.

From this foundation of faith, Brother Lawrence encouraged a complete surrender of ourselves to God, embracing both our earthly circumstances and our spiritual lives. He believed that our deepest satisfaction should be found in fulfilling God's will, whether our journey brought comfort or suffering, consolation or difficulty. A soul genuinely surrendered to God would learn to receive these changing circumstances without allowing its devotion to depend upon them. Such surrender did not require the disappearance of hardship, but a willingness to remain faithful through whatever God permitted.

He was especially concerned with those seasons when prayer becomes difficult and God's presence seems distant. At times, the soul may experience dryness, weariness, or an apparent absence of spiritual feeling, making the practice of prayer seem burdensome rather than joyful. Brother Lawrence understood these periods as trials through which love and faithfulness could be tested. He believed that such moments offered especially valuable opportunities to surrender ourselves to God, trusting that even a single sincere act of resignation might contribute greatly to our spiritual growth.

When our conversation turned toward the suffering, wrongdoing, and disorder of the world, Brother Lawrence expressed a perspective shaped by his understanding of human nature. He was not surprised by the sins and miseries he heard about each day; indeed, considering the harm of which human beings were capable, he sometimes wondered why there was not even more. His response was to pray for those involved and entrust the matter to God's providence. Believing that God possessed the power to remedy the evils of the world whenever He chose, Brother Lawrence did not allow himself to remain consumed by anxiety over circumstances beyond his control.

He also recognized that surrender to God required a growing awareness of the desires and passions moving within the human heart. These influences were not confined to obvious temptations or worldly pursuits, for they could become entangled even with our most sincere spiritual intentions. The desire to serve God might coexist with hidden attachments, personal ambitions, or motives we did not yet fully understand. Brother Lawrence believed that God would gradually illuminate these movements of the heart for anyone who genuinely desired to serve Him, allowing greater honesty and freedom to develop through attentive self-examination.

As our conversation drew to a close, Brother Lawrence extended a personal invitation. If my desire was sincerely to serve God, he assured me that I could return to speak with him as often as I wished, without any fear of becoming a burden. Yet he was equally clear that if I did not genuinely seek this purpose, there would be little reason for further visits. His invitation reflected the seriousness with which he approached the spiritual life, for he regarded communion with God as a matter worthy of our deepest sincerity and wholehearted attention.
