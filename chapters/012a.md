## Fourth Conversation — A Life of Continual Communion

*November 25, 1667*

Brother Lawrence spoke with great warmth and openness about his way of drawing near to God, returning to several things he had explained before. Everything, he said, began with a wholehearted willingness to relinquish whatever we recognized as drawing us away from God. Through this surrender, we could gradually become accustomed to a continual conversation with Him, approaching Him simply and without elaborate methods.

He encouraged recognizing God as intimately present and turning toward Him throughout the day. When we were uncertain about what to do, we could ask for help in understanding His will; when our duty was clear, we could ask for the grace to carry it out faithfully. Before beginning an action, we could offer it to God, and afterward give thanks. Within this familiar conversation, there would also be room to praise, adore, and love God for His infinite goodness and perfection.

Our sins, he believed, should never discourage us from asking for grace with complete confidence in the merits of Jesus Christ. He was convinced that God offered the assistance we needed in every action, and he described being distinctly aware of that help himself. When he failed to receive it, he attributed this to having wandered from the awareness of God's presence or forgotten to ask for assistance. He trusted that God would give light in uncertainty when a person's sincere desire was to please Him and act for His love.

Brother Lawrence explained that our growth in holiness depended less upon changing our occupations than upon changing the purpose for which we performed them. The things we ordinarily did for ourselves could become offerings to God. He regretted that so many people confused the means of spiritual growth with its goal, becoming attached to particular religious activities while allowing self-interest and concern for human approval to shape their conduct. The best approach he had discovered was to carry out ordinary responsibilities, as purely as possible, for the love of God.

He considered it a serious mistake to imagine that appointed times of prayer belonged to an entirely different kind of life from the hours spent working. During activity, we were called to remain close to God through our actions, just as we turned toward Him in formal prayer. For Brother Lawrence, prayer was an awareness of divine presence in which his soul became absorbed in God's love. When the appointed time ended, that relationship continued as he praised and blessed God amid his responsibilities.

He described his life as filled with continual joy, yet he also hoped that, when he had grown stronger, God would give him something to suffer. His trust included a willingness to receive hardship as well as consolation. He urged a wholehearted surrender of ourselves to God, resting in the assurance that God would never deceive those who entrusted themselves to Him.

He also encouraged perseverance in small acts of love. God, he said, regarded the love with which a task was performed rather than the greatness of the task itself. Frequent failures were to be expected at the beginning, when the practice was still unfamiliar. Over time, repeated efforts could become a settled habit, arising with increasing ease and bringing profound delight.

Brother Lawrence understood faith, hope, and love to contain the whole substance of religion. Through their practice, we became united to God's will; everything else had value insofar as it helped us toward that union. He spoke of faith making all things possible, hope making them less difficult, and love making them easier still. Perseverance in all three gradually deepened this freedom, directing us toward the fullest worship of God we could offer in this life and hoped to offer throughout eternity.

Alongside this joyful teaching, he spoke with severity about the need for self-knowledge and humility. He believed that an honest examination of ourselves would reveal such profound weakness and sin that we would recognize ourselves as deserving contempt and scarcely worthy of the name Christian. He pointed to our vulnerability to suffering, the countless circumstances that unsettle us, and the changes in health, mood, and inward disposition over which we have so little command. He understood the pains and labors of life, both inward and outward, as ways through which God humbled us.

From this perspective, he believed we should not be surprised when other people brought us trouble, temptation, resistance, or contradiction. He encouraged bearing these experiences for as long as God permitted them, regarding them as beneficial to the soul. The greater the holiness a person desired, he insisted, the more completely that person depended upon divine grace.

*The following recollections were gathered from other accounts of Brother Lawrence and included after the Fourth Conversation.*

When a member of his religious community, to whom he was obliged to speak openly, asked how he had acquired such a habitual awareness of God, Brother Lawrence traced it to his earliest days in the monastery. From the beginning, he had regarded God as the goal of all his thoughts and desires. Everything within him was to be directed toward God and find its fulfillment in Him.

During his novitiate, he spent the hours appointed for private prayer attending to God's reality and allowing that awareness to sink deeply into his heart. He relied more upon reverent affection than upon intricate reasoning or carefully constructed meditations. Through this simple approach, he cultivated the knowledge and love of God, resolving to live as continually as possible in His presence.

After prayer, he went to his assigned work in the kitchen, where he served as cook for the community. He first considered the responsibilities before him, giving thought to what needed to be done and when and how each task should be completed. He used the available intervals before and after his work for prayer. His attention to God accompanied a practical understanding of the work entrusted to him.

As he began, he would pray with the trust of a child speaking to a loving parent: “My God, You are here with me, and I must now give my attention to these outward duties in obedience to You. Grant me the grace to remain in Your presence as I work. Help me in what I undertake, receive everything I do, and hold all the affections of my heart.”

Throughout his work, he continued this familiar conversation with his Creator, asking for grace and offering each action to Him. When he finished, he considered how he had fulfilled his responsibilities. If he had done well, he gave thanks; if he had failed, he asked forgiveness. Then, without becoming discouraged, he renewed his intention and returned to the practice of God's presence as freely as though he had never wandered.

He explained that this freedom had developed through repeatedly getting up after his falls and renewing his acts of faith and love. What had once required considerable effort had gradually become familiar. Eventually, he found it as difficult to go without thinking of God as he had originally found it difficult to remember Him.

Having received so much comfort and blessing through this way of life, Brother Lawrence naturally recommended it earnestly to others. Yet those who observed him found his example more persuasive than his explanations. His face expressed a gentle, peaceful devotion, and even during the busiest hours in the kitchen he retained his inward attentiveness. He neither rushed nor delayed, but attended to each task at the proper time with a steady composure.

He described the experience simply: “My time of work is no different from my time of prayer. Amid the noise and clatter of the kitchen, with several people calling for different things at once, I remain with God in as much tranquility as if I were kneeling before the Blessed Sacrament.”
